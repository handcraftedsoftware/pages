I recently improved the landing page for one of my apps, Lightbox Tracing Pad.

The goal was not to make the page more decorative.

The goal was to make the product easier to understand:

- what it does;
- who it is for;
- why it is useful;
- what proof or trust signals matter;
- what action the visitor should take next.

This became a useful small case study in AI-assisted product work.

Not because AI "made the page."

Because the workflow made the work more structured, reviewable, and easier to finish.

## The starting point

Lightbox Tracing Pad is a simple app that turns an iPad or iPhone into a lightbox for tracing images on paper.

The original product page explained the app, but it was closer to a short description than a focused landing page.

It did not clearly guide the visitor through:

- the problem;
- the use cases;
- how the app works;
- privacy and trust;
- a clear App Store action.

That is a common issue with small product sites. The app exists, the link exists, but the page does not fully help a new visitor decide whether the app is for them.

## The workflow

Instead of asking an AI agent to "make the page better," I used a more structured workflow.

The work moved through several steps:

1. Define the goal.
2. Draft the page structure.
3. Review claims and privacy.
4. Prepare an implementation brief.
5. Build the page.
6. Check the result against the brief.
7. Capture evidence.

That structure mattered more than any single prompt.

## Step 1: define the product job

The first useful question was:

> What job should this page do?

For this page, the answer was:

> Help a visitor understand that Lightbox Tracing Pad turns an iPad or iPhone into a simple lightbox for tracing images on paper, then guide them to the App Store.

That gave the page a clear direction.

It was not a general portfolio page.
It was not an abstract creativity page.
It was not a long personal story.

It was a product page with one job.

## Step 2: turn the page into a sequence

The page structure became:

1. Hero
2. Problem
3. Who it is for
4. How it works
5. Features
6. Privacy
7. Maker note
8. Final App Store call to action

This sequence is simple, but it removed ambiguity.

The page no longer needed to invent a new shape. It needed to answer the right questions in the right order.

## Step 3: review claims before implementation

This was one of the most useful parts of the process.

Before implementation, I reviewed which claims were safe to make.

Allowed:

- the app imports images;
- the app helps prepare images for tracing;
- the app works on iPhone and iPad;
- the screen can be locked while tracing;
- the app does not collect data, aligned with the App Store privacy listing.

Not allowed:

- sales claims;
- download numbers;
- "best" or "professional" claims;
- conversion improvement claims;
- anything that implied measured business impact without evidence.

This kind of review is easy to skip when you are just trying to publish a page.

But it helps keep the page honest.

## Step 4: use public-safe visuals

For visuals, I used public App Store assets and neutral product screenshots.

That avoided a common privacy problem: accidentally using screenshots with personal images, private gallery content, documents, or other sensitive details.

The rule was simple:

> If the visual is already public and does not expose private material, it is safer to reuse than to improvise.

## Step 5: build with a clear brief

The implementation was not just "rewrite this page."

It had a brief:

- use the public product name: Lightbox Tracing Pad;
- do not show price on the site;
- include the approved hero line;
- include the required sections;
- use approved visuals;
- include App Store calls to action near the top and bottom;
- keep privacy and contact links accessible;
- do not add unsupported claims.

That made the build easier to check.

The brief became a contract between strategy, copy, implementation, and review.

## One concrete before and after

Before, the page mostly said what the app was.

After, the hero had to say why someone should care:

> Turn your iPad or iPhone into a simple lightbox for tracing images on paper.

That line does more work than a generic description.

It names the device.
It names the use case.
It names the physical workflow.
It helps the right visitor understand the app quickly.

That is the kind of copy change I want from AI-assisted product work: not louder, just clearer.

## Step 6: check the result

After implementation, the page was checked against the brief.

The review looked for concrete things:

- the approved product name;
- the hero positioning line;
- required page sections;
- App Store calls to action;
- absence of price text;
- privacy statement;
- no services or consulting pitch;
- contact and privacy links.

This is where AI-assisted work becomes much easier to trust.

The output is not accepted because it looks polished.

It is accepted because it matches the task, constraints, and evidence.

## What changed

The page moved from a short app description toward a focused product landing page.

It now explains:

- what the app does;
- who might use it;
- how it works;
- what features support the workflow;
- what privacy promise matters;
- where to download it.

That is a small change in scope, but a large change in clarity.

## What AI helped with

AI helped most with:

- turning a vague improvement idea into a structured task;
- generating page structure options;
- drafting copy;
- checking claims;
- noticing privacy risks;
- producing an implementation brief;
- reviewing the finished page against the brief.

The important part is that each step had boundaries.

AI did not decide what to publish.
AI did not invent results.
AI did not remove the need for review.

It helped make the work visible and easier to inspect.

## The lesson

The useful pattern was not "ask AI to improve a page."

The useful pattern was:

1. define the job;
2. create constraints;
3. draft;
4. review;
5. build;
6. verify;
7. record evidence.

That is slower than a single prompt.

It is also much easier to trust.

For me, this is where AI-assisted product work becomes useful: not when it replaces judgment, but when it creates a workflow where judgment has something concrete to inspect.
